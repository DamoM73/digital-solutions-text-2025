# Impacts 📝

:::{dropdown} Learning Goals
By the end of this section you will:
- understand and be able to describe a digital solution's personal, social and economic impacts (Unit 1)
- be able to evaluate the personal, social and economic impacts of storing data - including its security, privacy and ethical effects - from individual, organisational and government perspectives (Unit 2)
- be able to evaluate the personal, social and economic impacts of a generated digital solution, from different stakeholder perspectives (Unit 3)
- be able to evaluate the personal, social and economic impacts of data exchange, accuracy, ownership and emerging technologies, and the security impacts of a digital solution (Unit 4)
:::

The syllabus always groups impacts into the same three categories:

- personal impact
- social impact
- economic impact

What changes across the four units is not the categories themselves, but **what** you are evaluating the impacts of, and **how deeply** you are expected to evaluate them.

| Unit | Evaluate personal, social and economic impacts of... |
| :-- | :-- |
| Unit 1 | A digital solution, in general |
| Unit 2 | Storing data |
| Unit 3 | A digital solution you have actually generated, from different stakeholder perspectives |
| Unit 4 | Exchanging, and the accuracy and ownership of, data |

This page is organised the same way, so each section below only covers what is **new** at that point in the course - it assumes you already understand the sections above it.

## Unit 1: Understanding Impacts

In considering the user experience, you also need to consider the types of impacts the digital solution will have directly on the user and the wider community. Impacts can be both positive and negative, and considerations should be given to promoting the positive impacts and minimising the negative impacts.

### Personal Impacts

When evaluating a digital solution, several personal impacts should be considered to ensure the solution enhances individual well-being and does not introduce unintended negative consequences. The table below provides a summary.

| Impact Area | What to Consider |
| :-- | :-- |
| Personal Well-being and Lifestyle | Physical/mental health, stress, digital fatigue |
| Skills, Access, and Usability | Digital literacy, accessibility, user experience |
| Security, Privacy, and Safety | Data protection, cyber risks, confidentiality |
| Social and Ethical Considerations | Social connections, ethics, legal compliance |
| Economic and Practical Factors | Costs, sustainability, practicality |

**Personal Well-being and Lifestyle**

- **Health and Well-being:** Consider how the digital solution affects physical and mental health. This includes potential benefits such as improved access to information or services, as well as risks like increased screen time, stress, or exposure to harmful content.
- **Work-life Balance:** Evaluate whether the solution supports a healthy balance between work, study, and personal life, or if it could contribute to overwork or digital fatigue.
- **Subjective Well-being:** Assess the impact on personal happiness, satisfaction, and sense of purpose. Digital solutions can both enhance and diminish subjective well-being depending on their design and use.

**Skills, Access, and Usability**

- **Digital Literacy and Skills:** Determine if the individual has the necessary skills to use the solution effectively. Solutions should be accessible to users with varying levels of digital literacy.
- **Usability and User Experience:** Consider how intuitive and user-friendly the solution is. Poor usability can cause frustration and reduce adoption.
- **Accessibility:** Ensure the solution is usable by people with disabilities or those with limited access to technology.

**Security, Privacy, and Safety**

- **Personal Security:** Evaluate risks related to data breaches, cyberbullying, identity theft, and other security threats.
- **Privacy:** Assess how personal data is collected, stored, and shared. The solution should comply with privacy laws and respect user confidentiality.

**Social and Ethical Considerations**

- **Social Connections:** Consider whether the solution fosters positive social interactions or contributes to isolation and loneliness.
- **Ethical and Legal Compliance:** Ensure the solution aligns with ethical standards and legal requirements, such as avoiding defamation and ensuring inclusivity.

**Economic and Practical Factors**

- **Cost and Economic Impact:** Consider personal financial implications, such as purchase costs, subscription fees, or potential savings from increased efficiency.
- **Practicality and Sustainability:** Evaluate if the solution is sustainable for long-term personal use, including maintenance and ongoing support requirements.

### Social Impacts

When evaluating a digital solution, consider how it affects social equity, inclusion, well-being, and the broader structure of society. The goal is to ensure technology supports a fair, connected, and healthy community.

| Social Impact Area | What to Consider |
| :-- | :-- |
| Equity & Inclusion | Access across social groups, bridging the digital divide, support for marginalised users |
| Community & Culture | Effects on relationships, collaboration, trust, and social cohesion |
| Well-being & Safety | Mental health, cyberbullying, digital overload, privacy, and data security |
| Economic Participation | Job creation or loss, access to education, skills development, and workforce changes |
| Unintended Societal Effects | Surveillance, isolation, algorithmic bias, and systemic inequality |

**Equity & Inclusion**

Digital solutions should be accessible and usable by all people, regardless of socioeconomic status, ability, age, language, or location.

- Does the solution enable fair access to technology and opportunities?
- Is it usable by people with different abilities, incomes, languages, or digital literacy levels?

**Community & Culture**

Digital solutions influence how people communicate, form communities, and trust each other.

- Does the solution strengthen social connections and community engagement?
- Could it increase polarisation, mistrust, or social isolation?

**Well-being & Safety**

Digital solutions must protect users from harm and promote healthy usage.

- Are there risks of digital addiction, cyberbullying, or harm to mental health?
- Does the solution protect user privacy and build trust through strong data practices?

**Economic Participation**

Digital tools can support or disrupt employment, education, and economic opportunity.

- Will the solution create or eliminate jobs?
- Does it promote learning and digital skill development across all sectors of society?

**Unintended Societal Effects**

- Could the solution lead to negative systemic outcomes, such as surveillance, exclusion, or reinforcing social inequalities?

### Economic Impacts

Digital solutions can reshape economies by influencing productivity, employment, trade, and long-term economic resilience. Evaluating these impacts helps ensure solutions support sustainable growth, competitiveness, and inclusive development.

**Key Economic Impact Areas**

| Impact Area | What to Consider |
| :-- | :-- |
| Economic Productivity | Efficiency gains, automation, resource optimisation across industries |
| Employment & Workforce | Job creation, displacement, digital skill demand, workforce transformation |
| Market Growth & Trade | Domestic and international expansion, digital trade, competitiveness |
| Inclusion & Access | Access to financial and digital services, support for underserved populations |
| Resilience & Innovation | Ability to adapt to disruptions, encourage entrepreneurship and new industries |
| Public Revenue & Policy | Taxation models, regulatory needs, economic policy alignment |
| Return on Investment | Cost-benefit analysis, sustainability of implementation and maintenance |

**Economic Productivity**

- Does the solution reduce costs and improve efficiency in key economic sectors?
- Consider gains in automation, streamlined logistics, and more effective resource use that boost national productivity.

**Employment & Workforce**

- Will it create new roles (e.g. in tech or services) or displace existing jobs through automation?
- Evaluate the need for reskilling programs and the balance between job loss and creation.

**Market Growth & Trade**

- Can it enable local firms to access new markets or compete globally through digital trade?
- Digital tools can lower barriers for small businesses and enhance export potential for services.

**Inclusion & Access**

- Does the solution expand access to essential services like banking, education, or government platforms?
- Broader access boosts participation in the formal economy and reduces inequality.

**Resilience & Innovation**

- Can the economy better withstand shocks (e.g. pandemics, supply chain failures) through this solution?
- Does it foster entrepreneurship, new business models, or scalable innovations?

**Public Revenue & Policy**

- How will digitalisation affect tax bases (e.g. e-commerce, gig economy)?
- Consider the need for updated regulations to ensure fair taxation and economic oversight.

**Return on Investment**

- Do the long-term benefits (e.g. efficiency, exports, job growth) outweigh the upfront investment?
- Include costs of infrastructure, training, and system maintenance in the analysis.

## Unit 2: Impacts of Storing Data

Unit 2 keeps the same three impact categories from Unit 1, but narrows the focus onto **data** specifically. When you evaluate the personal, social and economic impacts of storing data, you also need to identify the **effect** involved (security, privacy or ethical) and **whose perspective** you are considering (individual, organisational or government).

| Perspective | Example |
| :-- | :-- |
| Individual | A student whose attendance records are stored in a school database |
| Organisational | A gym storing member health and payment details |
| Government | A department storing citizens' tax or health records |

### Personal Impacts of Storing Data

- **Security effect:** if a database is poorly secured, an individual's stored data can be stolen, leading to identity theft, financial loss or targeted scams.
- **Privacy effect:** individuals often do not know exactly what data organisations or governments store about them, or who can see it. Storing more data than is needed increases the risk that private details are exposed or misused.
- **Ethical effect:** individuals may have little real choice about whether their data is stored, especially where a service is essential (e.g. enrolling in school, opening a bank account). This raises questions of consent and fairness.

### Social Impacts of Storing Data

- **Security effect:** a breach at an organisation or government department can undermine public trust in institutions more broadly, not just the individuals directly affected.
- **Privacy effect:** the decisions organisations and governments make about what data to collect, and who can access it, shape privacy norms and expectations across the whole community.
- **Ethical effect:** data matching between organisations, or across government departments, can disproportionately affect particular communities, including vulnerable or marginalised groups' access to services.

### Economic Impacts of Storing Data

- **Security effect:** a data breach can cost an organisation or government significant money in fines, remediation, legal penalties and lost trade.
- **Privacy effect:** complying with privacy obligations has a direct cost for organisations and governments (systems, staff, audits) - while the stored data itself also has economic value.
- **Ethical effect:** organisations may profit from data collected about individuals, without those individuals sharing in that value, raising fairness concerns about who benefits economically from stored data.

:::{tip} Stop and Think
:class: dropdown
When you evaluate a digital solution for an assessment task, do not just describe the impact - state which category it belongs to (personal, social or economic), which effect is involved (security, privacy or ethical) and **who** is affected (individual, organisation or government). This makes your evaluation specific and measurable, rather than broad or generic.
:::

## Unit 3: Evaluating the Impacts of a Solution

Units 1 and 2 asked you to **explore and describe** possible personal, social and economic impacts before a solution exists. Unit 3 changes what you do with these same three categories: once you have generated an actual prototype digital solution, you must **evaluate** its real impacts against the real-world problem it was built for, from different stakeholder perspectives.

### Personal, Social and Economic Impacts of a Generated Solution

- **Personal:** e.g. a biometric login might improve security for individual users, but reduce accessibility for users without a compatible device.
- **Social:** e.g. a communication app might strengthen engagement for some groups, while excluding families without reliable internet access.
- **Economic:** e.g. automating a manual process might reduce operating costs for the organisation, but eliminate certain jobs.

Unit 3 also introduces innovation as a source of personal, social and economic impact. New or re-imagined digital solutions can create opportunities as well as impacts:

- **Personal opportunities:** new ways to save time, connect with others, or access services
- **Social opportunities:** ways an innovative solution could benefit the wider community
- **Economic (business) opportunities:** new markets, products or ways of operating that innovation makes possible

Emerging technologies (e.g. machine learning, deep learning, neural networks, natural language processing) are a common driver of innovation, and can introduce personal, social and economic impacts of their own, on top of the impacts of the solution itself.

:::{tip} Exploring vs Evaluating
:class: dropdown
- **Exploring/describing impacts (Units 1 and 2):** "This type of solution could affect users' privacy."
- **Evaluating impacts (Unit 3):** "This solution's use of biometric login improved account security for users, but reduced accessibility for users without compatible devices, which disadvantaged lower-income households."

Notice the second example is specific to the actual solution and names who is affected.
:::

## Unit 4: Impacts of Data Exchange and Emerging Technology

Unit 4 keeps the same personal, social and economic categories, and widens the scope of Unit 2's data impacts again. Unit 2 focused on the impacts of **storing** data. Unit 4 adds the impacts of **exchanging** data, and considers its **accuracy** and **ownership**, as well as the impacts of emerging technologies and the availability of public and private data.

### Personal Impacts of Data Exchange

- **Exchange:** data intercepted while moving between systems can directly harm the individual it is about (e.g. test results sent to the wrong recipient).
- **Accuracy:** if exchanged data about an individual is inaccurate or out of date, decisions may be made about that person using wrong information.
- **Ownership:** once data about an individual has been exchanged, it can become unclear who is responsible for correcting or removing it.
- **Emerging technology:** systems using AI to process exchanged data may make automated decisions about an individual without their knowledge.

### Social Impacts of Data Exchange

- **Exchange:** linking datasets between organisations or government departments can enable more efficient services for communities, but also new forms of surveillance.
- **Public and private data:** publicly available datasets can create positive impacts through transparency and research, but can expose sensitive information and damage community trust if not properly de-identified. Restricting data as private protects individuals, but can limit transparency or accountability if over-restricted.
- **Emerging technology:** growing reliance on automated decision-making using exchanged data raises questions about fairness and bias affecting particular groups in society.

### Economic Impacts of Data Exchange

- **Exchange:** enabling systems to share data can reduce costs and improve efficiency for organisations and governments, e.g. not having to re-enter the same data multiple times.
- **Ownership:** unclear data ownership can create legal and financial risk for organisations when exchanged data is misused or disputed.
- **Security:** as data is exchanged and solutions become more interactive, the potential economic cost of a security breach grows, since more systems and organisations are exposed.

When evaluating a solution's impacts at this level, you are expected to connect the personal, social and economic impacts of data security and privacy directly to the real-world problem and the solution you generated, taking into account changes in interactivity and how data is created, used and shared - not just describe them as general concerns.
